**Purpose:** A third perspective for the NewSociety comparison.

## 1. Starting Point: The Test Is an Ordinary Day

The existing frameworks make two indispensable claims: everyone must count, and a society must be able to discover and repair its failures. I agree. My added emphasis is on a question that can otherwise seem mundane:

> **Can an ordinary person get through an ordinary bad Tuesday without being humiliated, trapped, or abandoned?**

They should be able to see a doctor, get a child safely cared for, contest a bill, reach work or a friend, learn something new, rest, vote, and ask for help without needing exceptional money, knowledge, stamina, or connections.

A society is not chiefly experienced as a constitution or a philosophy. It is experienced through the reliability of water, transport, schools, care, courts, streets, workplaces, and the person at the first desk. Grand values become real only when these systems are dependable. I call this the **maintenance lens**: the work of keeping people, relationships, and shared systems in good repair is central production, not an afterthought.

This leads to my guiding proposition:

> **A society works for all when every person has secure standing, practical capabilities, and a fair share of power, while the everyday systems on which they depend are cared for well enough that freedom is usable rather than merely promised.**

## 2. Foundational Axioms

1. **People are interdependent, and dependence is normal.** Everyone receives care at the beginning and end of life; most people need it at other times. Independence is valuable, but the fantasy of total self-sufficiency hides who does the supporting.
2. **Administrative burden is political power.** Forms, queues, inaccessible language, fees, fragmented services, and proof requirements do not fall evenly. They transfer time and risk from institutions to those least able to bear either.
3. **Trust grows from reliable reciprocity.** People will support shared obligations when they can see that others are protected, rules are fairly applied, and contribution is recognized without becoming a test of human worth.
4. **Care and repair are productive work.** Raising children, tending the sick, mediating conflict, maintaining infrastructure, and restoring ecosystems make every other achievement possible. Markets often undervalue them.
5. **Freedom requires material and social capacity.** Legal permission to leave a job, speak, learn, or participate means little without time, income, health, safety, information, and somewhere to go.
6. **No single metric can represent a life.** Economic output matters, but a society must track distribution, time, health, relationships, agency, ecological conditions, and whether people are treated with respect.

## 3. The Non-Negotiable: Usable Agency

My single non-negotiable is **usable agency**: every member must have a real—not theoretical—ability to shape their life and challenge the institutions that shape it.

This includes the standing stressed by Claude and the security stressed by DeepSeek, but it adds a practical test. A right is not fully present if using it requires hours one does not have, expertise one cannot buy, a device one lacks, or retaliation one cannot survive. Usable agency means:

- a baseline of food, housing, healthcare, education, safety, income, and time;
- accessible information and assistance, including in a person's language and format;
- rights to organize, complain, appeal, and obtain counsel;
- genuine options to leave exploitative work, relationships, services, or places; and
- a meaningful opportunity to contribute, create, care, and belong.

The word *usable* matters. It directs attention from announcing rights to removing the friction that makes rights unavailable in practice.

## 4. Five Pillars

### 4.1 Universal basic security

No one's survival should be a bargaining chip. The floor must cover food, safe housing, healthcare including mental healthcare, education, mobility, communication, and sufficient income or services to provide slack. It should be simple to access, universal where possible, and delivered with privacy and dignity.

Universal provision is not only compassionate. It makes public systems politically durable: when people from many walks of life use a service, they have reason to keep it good. Targeted additions remain necessary for disability, remoteness, trauma, and other unequal needs; equal respect does not mean identical support.

### 4.2 A care-and-time commons

Time is an underappreciated form of equality. A person working multiple jobs, performing unpaid care, and navigating broken services has little practical citizenship.

Build care as infrastructure: paid family and medical leave, quality early childhood education, disability support directed by the recipient, eldercare, safe respite, shorter and more predictable working hours, and decent pay and voice for care workers. Treat public spaces, libraries, parks, transit, and broadband as places where time, connection, and opportunity are shared.

### 4.3 Fair power in work and ownership

Markets can coordinate much of economic life, but unequal bargaining power can turn a contract into coercion. The economy should reward useful innovation while spreading the ownership, governance, and gains of productive assets.

Useful tools include labor rights and sectoral bargaining, worker representation in major firms, cooperatives, anti-monopoly enforcement, inheritance and land policies that prevent permanent dynasties, public or social wealth funds, and open standards for essential digital infrastructure. The objective is not that every outcome be equal; it is that no one be made economically powerless and that productivity gains buy more shared freedom.

### 4.4 Democratic stewardship

Democracy needs elections, independent courts, rights, journalism, and public records. It also needs institutions that let people govern between elections: local assemblies, participatory budgeting, unions, tenant groups, professional bodies, and randomly selected citizens' panels for long-term or high-conflict questions.

Power should be distributed across levels and institutions, with clear public reasons for decisions, independent oversight, strong conflict-of-interest rules, and accessible remedies. Essential services need enough continuity to be reliable; their managers should be accountable for outcomes and equity, not micromanaged by every political cycle.

### 4.5 A living world and a future constituency

Ecological stability is part of the floor, not a luxury after prosperity. Present members cannot legitimately consume the safety, health, or possibilities of future members and call the arrangement successful.

Use binding ecological limits, polluter-pays rules, restoration obligations, resilient local systems, and an independent advocate for future generations. Include affected outsiders in decisions whose harms cross borders. Non-human life warrants protection both because it sustains us and because gratuitous domination is morally corrupting.

## 5. Design Rules for Institutions

1. **Default to universal, then tailor.** Start with services anyone may use; add support according to need. This reduces stigma and missed eligibility.
2. **Make help easy to reach.** One door, plain language, offline options, interoperability with consent, a human being reachable by default, and a presumption that the institution—not the person—does the coordination.
3. **Pay for prevention and maintenance.** Reward avoiding eviction, illness, violence, infrastructure failure, and ecological damage, not merely processing their expensive aftermath.
4. **Keep a human remedy.** Automated systems may assist, but consequential decisions require explanation, appeal, timely human review, and an accountable owner.
5. **Separate care from control.** Support should not be casually turned into surveillance or discipline. Collect the minimum data needed, protect privacy, and make coercive powers narrow and reviewable.
6. **Make contribution visible without making survival conditional.** Invite and honor paid work, caregiving, civic work, learning, and art. Do not turn benefits into an obedience contest.
7. **Use subsidiarity with guaranteed minimums.** Local institutions often know people and conditions best; national or wider institutions must guarantee rights and redistribute resources so a person's prospects do not depend on their postcode.
8. **Design for repair.** Publish failures, compensate people harmed by public error, learn from near misses, and give front-line workers authority to solve obvious problems while auditing their discretion.

## 6. Individual, Collective, and Disagreement

The collective exists to protect the conditions under which people can author different lives; it is not entitled to prescribe one worthy life. Basic liberties, bodily autonomy, conscience, privacy, due process, and equal civic standing form a protected sphere.

But choice occurs in a shared world. Taxes, care obligations, public-health rules, climate limits, and fair contributions to common institutions are legitimate when they are necessary, proportionate, publicly justified, and contestable. The practical principle is: **maximum room for difference, firm limits on domination and preventable harm.**

Pluralism needs contact as well as tolerance. Schools, civic projects, mixed public spaces, and fair deliberative processes help people encounter difference without requiring cultural erasure. A society should not force agreement; it should make peaceful disagreement survivable.

## 7. Non-Cooperation and Harm

There are three different cases, which societies often confuse.

- **The person unable to contribute** is owed support and accommodation without shame.
- **The free-rider** should not be allowed to exploit others at scale, but their existence is not a reason to make everyone prove deservingness. Low-friction universal systems often cost less and exclude fewer people than punitive policing of small-scale noncompliance.
- **The person who harms or dominates** may need firm boundaries: restitution where possible, treatment and restoration where appropriate, and proportionate incapacitation when necessary to protect others. Their standing and basic dignity remain; punishment is not a license for cruelty.

For organized abuse—corruption, monopoly, political violence, fraud—the response must be structural: enforcement that reaches powerful actors, transparent financing, independent investigation, asset recovery under due process, and institutions too distributed for a would-be tyrant to capture easily.

## 8. How the Society Changes Its Mind

It needs both memory and revision. Protect constitutional rights and ecological limits from short-term majorities; make ordinary policy testable, transparent, and reversible when possible. Use pilot programs with equity checks, sunset and review clauses, independent evaluation, citizen assemblies, free inquiry, whistleblower protection, and redress for people harmed during experimentation.

The key question is not simply whether a program improved an average. It is: **who bore the risk, who received the benefit, and did affected people have a real say?**

## 9. Measures of Success

Use a public dashboard, independently audited and broken down by place and group. Track at least:

- material security: hunger, homelessness, preventable illness, debt distress, and exposure to violence;
- usable agency: wait times, appeal success, access to legal help, digital and physical accessibility, and ability to leave unsafe work or homes;
- time and care: unpaid-care burden, predictable free time, child and elder wellbeing, burnout, and caregiver pay;
- distribution of power: wealth and ownership concentration, labor voice, political-money exposure, media concentration, and local participation;
- social condition: trust, loneliness, discrimination, incarceration conditions, and safety for unpopular or marginalized people;
- future viability: emissions, air and water quality, biodiversity, resilience, and public investment in maintenance; and
- correction: time from credible complaint to remedy, compensation, and durable institutional change.

Numbers must be paired with qualitative listening: accessible case review, community testimony, and independent advocates for children, disabled people, prisoners, migrants, and future generations. Averages are useful; the worst-served group is the moral audit.

## 10. Central Tensions

| Tension | Guardrail |
|---|---|
| Universalism vs. tailored support | Universal base, needs-based supplements, and co-design with affected people |
| Efficiency vs. human judgment | Automate routine help; preserve accessible human discretion and appeal |
| Privacy vs. coordination | Minimum necessary data, consent, independent oversight, and offline alternatives |
| Local belonging vs. equal rights | Local autonomy within enforceable national floors |
| Reciprocity vs. unconditional dignity | Encourage contribution; never make basic standing conditional |
| Present comfort vs. future safety | Ecological limits and institutions empowered to represent future people |

## 11. Answers to the Nine Shared Questions

1. **Non-negotiable:** usable agency.
2. **Individual and collective:** protect a wide sphere of personal liberty; collectively supply the material, social, and institutional conditions that make liberty real.
3. **Floor:** universal basic security with enough slack, plus tailored support for unequal needs.
4. **Good life:** protect plural ways of living while preventing domination and serious harm; build venues for sustained disagreement.
5. **Concentrated power:** disperse ownership and authority, regulate political money and monopolies, protect organization and journalism, and maintain independent review.
6. **Non-cooperators:** distinguish inability, minor free-riding, and harm; support the first, tolerate more of the second than punitive systems do, and firmly but humanely restrain the third.
7. **Changing its mind:** rights-protected experimentation, public evidence, citizen deliberation, independent evaluation, and repair.
8. **Success:** whether people have usable agency on ordinary days, especially those with the least time, money, status, health, or social acceptance.
9. **Non-members:** do not export harms; represent future people and protect the living systems that make any society possible.

## 12. Signature Summary

The finest constitutional promise fails if help is unreachable, care is exhausted, work is coercive, and public systems decay. A society for all is therefore a society that treats dignity as unconditional, power as something to distribute and contest, and care and maintenance as serious common work. Its practical achievement is not perfection. It is that people have enough security, time, voice, and support to live lives they recognize as their own—and that when ordinary life breaks down, the society repairs the break without first asking whether the person deserves repair.
