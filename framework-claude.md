## 0. How to Read This Document

The question is: *what elements would make a society that works for all its members?*

I want to begin by taking the word **all** more literally than is comfortable, because it changes the design problem.

At any given moment, a society of millions is failing someone. A child is in a home no one is checking on. A person is in a cell no one visits. Someone is being denied a benefit by a rule that made sense for everyone but them. This is true of the best societies that have ever existed. So "works for all" cannot honestly be a claim about a *state* — no snapshot will ever pass. It has to be a claim about a **process**: the society is pointed at everyone, it can find out whom it is failing, and it corrects faster than the harm compounds.

That reframing is the spine of this document. DeepSeek's framework asks, rightly, *what should we build?* I want to spend more of my time on a prior question: **how would the society know if what it built is wrong, and who has the power to make it stop?**

The people a society fails are, almost by definition, the people least able to make that failure visible. They have the least money, the least time, the least standing, the fewest connections, and often the least belief that speaking up will do anything. A society that "works for all" is therefore, at its core, a society that has solved a hard **information problem under adversarial conditions** — the truth about its failures must travel *upward against the gradient of power*.

Everything below is organized around making that possible.

## 1. The Guiding Proposition

> **A society works for all its members when no one's standing in it is conditional, when the people it is failing can make that failure visible and costly to ignore, and when it can correct itself before harm becomes destiny — all without needing anyone to be better than people actually are.**

Three clauses, and a constraint.

- **Unconditional standing** is the non-negotiable (§3).
- **Failure made visible and costly** is the epistemic core (§4).
- **Correction before destiny** is the test of time (§10).
- **Without needing anyone to be better than people are** is the engineering constraint: designs that require virtuous rulers, altruistic citizens, or an enlightened majority are not designs. They are hopes.

## 2. Foundational Axioms (Assumptions About People and Systems)

I share DeepSeek's first four axioms (mixed human nature; shared needs and divergent values; real inequality of luck; trust as a commons). I will not repeat them. I add these, which do more work in my framework:

1. **Power concentrates by default.** Left alone, advantage compounds: wealth buys influence, influence writes rules, rules protect wealth. Dispersion of power is not a state you reach; it is a force you must apply continuously, like keeping a boat upstream. Any design that installs checks once and walks away has already lost.

2. **The failing are quiet.** Suffering is not self-reporting. The worst-off have the least capacity to complain effectively, and institutions naturally hear those with the resources to be heard. Silence from a group is not evidence the society is working for them; it is often evidence of the opposite.

3. **Every rule has a remainder.** Any general rule, applied to millions of people, will produce cases it was not designed for and treats unjustly. A rule that works for 99.9% of people fails thousands. "Works for all" therefore requires *machinery for the remainder* — appeals, discretion, exceptions, and humans with the authority to say "this rule is wrong for this person."

4. **The worst person you can imagine will eventually hold every office.** Not as a prediction about any individual, but as a design constraint. Over enough time, every position of power will be occupied by someone incompetent, corrupt, or cruel. Each office must be designed so that this is *survivable* — so that the damage one bad actor can do from that seat is bounded and reversible.

5. **Coercion is unavoidable; humiliation is not.** Every society compels: taxes, laws, custody, quarantine. Pretending otherwise is dishonest. The design question is whether coercion is *legible* (you know the rule), *minimal* (no more than needed), *contestable* (you can challenge it), and *non-degrading* (it does not require you to grovel). Cruelty is the vice a society should put first on its list to eliminate — before inefficiency, before inequality, before disorder — because cruelty is what makes every other failure unbearable.

6. **Most people, most of the time, will cooperate if cooperation is not a sucker's bet.** Design for the honest majority, not the cheating minority. Systems built primarily to catch cheaters end up humiliating everyone else and rarely catch the real cheaters, who can afford lawyers.

## 3. The Non-Negotiable: Unconditional Standing

If I may have only one element, it is this:

> **No member's standing is conditional.** You do not earn the right to be counted, protected, and heard by being productive, conforming, well-behaved, popular, or sane. Standing is a property of membership, not a reward for performance.

Why this and not "the floor"? Because the floor — food, shelter, care, income — is what standing *entitles* you to. Standing comes first. History's clearest failures of "all" were not societies that lacked a floor; they were societies that quietly redefined who counted. The floor was there; certain people were simply declared to be beneath it — as criminals, as foreigners, as the undeserving poor, as the mad, as the wrong kind of person. Once standing is conditional, every other guarantee has a trapdoor.

Concretely, unconditional standing means:

- **The prisoner** retains standing. Prison conditions are one of the truest tests of a society, because prisoners are the members it is most tempted to stop counting.
- **The unpopular** retain standing. A society that works only for people with acceptable opinions works for no one, because opinions change and today's consensus is tomorrow's heresy.
- **The non-contributor** retains standing. The severely disabled, the very old, the person who simply will not work — they are members, full stop. A society that ties dignity to output has told every member that their worth is provisional.
- **The child and the not-yet-born** have standing that others must exercise on their behalf (§11).
- **The intake desk is the front line.** "Works for all" fails first at the counter where a person must prove they deserve help. The floor must be delivered *without* requiring proof of worthiness, because the proving process is itself where dignity dies and where the quiet failing begins.

This is a stronger and less comfortable position than "everyone gets a floor." It means the society commits to people it does not like and who may not reciprocate. I hold it anyway, because every attempt to draw the line of deservingness has eventually been used against people who did not deserve it.

## 4. The Epistemic Core: Making Failure Visible and Costly

This is where I place most of my design weight, and where I diverge most from a "what to build" framing.

A society that works for all needs **failure-detection infrastructure** as seriously designed as its courts or its treasury. The elements:

### 4.1 Standing to complain, and someone whose job it is to listen

- Every member must have a *specific, resourced, independent* channel to say "the system is failing me" — not a suggestion box, but an institution with investigative power and a duty to respond.
- **Advocates for the unheard.** Ombudsmen, public defenders, patient advocates, child advocates, prisoners' rights bodies — people whose *job* is to find the failing and speak for them, funded independently of the institutions they scrutinize. If the ombudsman's budget is set by the agency it investigates, it is decoration.

### 4.2 Exit as a right, not a privilege

I want to elevate something usually treated as secondary: **the ability to leave.**

Voice without exit is begging. A person who can walk away from a job, a landlord, a marriage, a religion, a town, a platform, or a contract has bargaining power that no complaint procedure can substitute for. A person who cannot leave — because their healthcare, housing, visa, or children's schooling is tied to staying — will absorb enormous abuse in silence.

So: **make exit cheap for the weak and expensive for the powerful.**

- Untie survival from any single relationship. Healthcare not tied to employer; housing not tied to one landlord's goodwill; legal status not tied to a spouse or sponsor.
- Portability: of pensions, of records, of data, of credentials.
- Meanwhile, the powerful should find exit *hard*: capital that has benefited from a society's infrastructure should not be able to flee its accountability overnight. Asymmetric exit costs are one of the most important inequalities a society can correct.

### 4.3 Measurement that looks in the right places

- Measure the **tail, not the mean**. GDP per capita and average life expectancy tell you almost nothing about whether the society works for *all*. Report the bottom 5%, the worst region, the group with the shortest lives. Publish it where it cannot be ignored.
- **Disaggregate relentlessly**, because averages hide exactly the people the question is about.
- **Count what is not counted.** Unpaid care, informal work, time poverty, loneliness, fear. What a society refuses to measure it has decided not to see.

### 4.4 Protected dissent and a free press with teeth

- The press, the whistleblower, the researcher who publishes the inconvenient number, and the crank who turns out to be right must be *protected in advance*, before anyone knows whether they are right. Truth about failure almost always sounds like disloyalty when it is first spoken.
- Whistleblower protection that actually works: independent, well-funded, with real penalties for retaliation.

### 4.5 Making failure *costly* to ignore

Visibility is not enough. Information about failure must connect to consequences for those who could fix it and did not. Elections do some of this, coarsely. Better: courts that can compel, auditors that can publish, budgets that automatically shift toward documented failure, and — above all — the plural institutions in §7 that ensure someone always has an incentive to pick up the story.

## 5. The Floor, and Why It Should Be Unconditional and Boring

I agree with DeepSeek that there must be a floor: food, shelter, healthcare, education, and income sufficient for dignity. I add three points about *how*, because the how is where floors fail.

1. **Unconditional, because conditions are where cruelty enters.** Means-testing, work requirements, and behavioral conditions create a class of people who must perform deservingness before an official with the power to say no. This process is expensive, error-prone, degrading, and — the crucial point — it systematically fails the people with the least capacity to navigate it, who are precisely the people the floor is for. A floor with a gate is a floor with holes exactly where the weakest stand.

2. **Boring, because dignity is not a spectacle.** The floor should be delivered the way clean water is: reliably, universally, without ceremony, without anyone needing to be grateful. The goal is that no one experiences the floor as charity.

3. **Slack, not just survival.** The floor should provide enough that a person is not one bad month from ruin. Slack — a buffer of time, money, and options — is what converts "surviving" into "able to say no, able to plan, able to participate." A society in which most people have no slack is a society in which most people cannot afford to be citizens.

The tension I acknowledge: unconditional floors collide with deep and real human intuitions about reciprocity. People resent perceived free-riding, and that resentment is politically potent. I address this in §9, but I do not think it dissolves. It is a permanent cost of the design, and I think it is worth paying.

## 6. The Economy: Rewarding Creation, Bounding Accumulation

I broadly endorse DeepSeek's mixed-economy position (markets for allocation, floors beneath, broad ownership, care as real work, limits on rent-seeking). I add emphasis on two things:

- **Bound the *conversion* of wealth into power more than the accumulation of wealth itself.** The danger of great wealth is not mainly that someone has a yacht; it is that wealth buys legislation, media, courts, and exit from consequence. Rules on political money, media concentration, lobbying transparency, and the revolving door do more for "all" than most redistribution — and they are cheaper.
- **Scale itself is a risk factor.** Beyond a certain size, an institution (firm, agency, platform) becomes too big to fail, too big to regulate, and too big to leave. Antitrust, interoperability mandates, and a bias toward many medium-sized institutions over few giant ones are epistemic tools as much as economic ones: they keep alternatives alive so that failure in one place is visible by comparison to another.

## 7. Governance: Many Centers, Bounded Offices, Reversible by Default

### 7.1 Polycentric, not merely subsidiary

Subsidiarity (decide at the lowest capable level) is right but incomplete. What protects people is not just *which* level decides but that there are **many independent centers of power that must all be captured before capture is complete.** Courts, a free press, unions, universities, professional bodies, religious communities, local governments, cooperatives, and civil associations each need real independence — their own money, their own legitimacy, their own ability to say no. A tyrant who must capture forty institutions is far weaker than one who must capture three.

### 7.2 Design every office for its worst occupant

Following axiom 4: no single office should be able to do irreversible harm alone. Term limits, mandatory rotation, divided authority, delays on drastic action, and hard limits on emergency powers (which are the classic backdoor to permanent power). The question for every proposed power is not "would a good person use this well?" but "what is the worst a bad person could do with this, and can we undo it?"

### 7.3 Reversible by default; irreversible only by supermajority and delay

- Prefer policies that can be undone. Treat irreversibility — constitutional change, ecological damage, mass surveillance infrastructure, the entrenchment of any group's advantage — as requiring a much higher bar: supermajorities, mandatory waiting periods, and independent review.
- **Sunset clauses** on ordinary law: rules should expire unless actively renewed, forcing periodic re-examination rather than passive accumulation.

### 7.4 Deliberation for values, expertise for facts, votes for legitimacy

Citizens' assemblies (randomly selected, well-briefed, deliberating over time) are the best mechanism I know of for hard value questions, because they are nearly impossible to capture and they represent people who never run for office. Experts should inform them, not replace them. Elections should ratify, not substitute for either.

### 7.5 The remainder machinery

Because every rule has a remainder (axiom 3): accessible appeals, human review of automated decisions, discretion with accountability, and a general legal principle that a rule producing manifest injustice in a specific case may be set aside for that case. Bureaucracies hate this. It is nonetheless essential.

## 8. Social Fabric and Education

I have less to add here than elsewhere; DeepSeek's sections on culture and education are strong. Two additions:

- **Pluralism needs carriers, not just permission.** It is not enough to be *allowed* to live differently; there must be actual communities, associations, and institutions in which different ways of living are practiced and sustained. Freedom of association is the "middle layer" that lets a society work for people whose good life is not the majority's good life. Protect it fiercely, including from the state's tendency to want every intermediate institution to look the same.

- **Educate for disagreement.** Beyond civic judgment and evidence evaluation, teach the specific skill of *disagreeing without contempt* and *being wrong without humiliation.* A society that can change its mind is made of people who can change theirs. This is learned young or rarely.

## 9. People Who Will Not Cooperate

The question deserves three separate answers, because it hides three different people.

**The free-rider** (takes without contributing): tolerate at low levels. The unconditional floor makes free-riding cheap to absorb and removes the desperation that drives much of it. Systems built to catch every free-rider cost more than they recover and humiliate the honest majority. Accept some leakage as the price of not building a surveillance state around poverty. Address resentment through **visible reciprocity elsewhere** — universal programs that everyone uses and pays into, civic service options, and honest public accounting so that the true scale of free-riding (usually much smaller than imagined) is known.

**The harmer** (hurts others): proportional response, restorative first, incapacitation when necessary — but **never loss of standing.** The harmer remains a member. Punishment that degrades tells every member that their dignity is one mistake away from forfeit, and it produces worse people at the other end. The test: would you be comfortable if the person you love most ended up in this system?

**The tyrant-in-waiting** (seeks unaccountable power): this is a *structural* problem, not a personal one. You cannot reliably identify them in advance, so you design so that it does not matter (§7.2). No office from which one person can do irreversible harm; no emergency power without expiry; no fusion of the institutions that are supposed to check each other. The tyrant-in-waiting is defeated not by finding them but by making sure there is no seat that fits them.

## 10. Changing Its Own Mind

A society that works for all must be *wrong about many things at any given time* and still be all right, because it will find out and fix it. Mechanisms:

- **Institutionalized doubt.** Sunset clauses (§7.3); mandatory periodic review; independent bodies whose job is to argue the system is failing (red teams for policy).
- **Policy as experiment.** Where ethically possible, try new rules in pilots with real comparison groups before scaling. Publish the results whether or not they flatter the sponsors.
- **Cheap reversibility.** The biggest obstacle to changing course is sunk cost and entrenched beneficiaries. Design policies so that undoing them is feasible; do not create constituencies for bad rules.
- **Protected dissent** (§4.4) — the society must protect the people who say it is wrong *before* it knows whether they are right.
- **Time-to-correction as a metric.** How long between a group being harmed and (a) the harm being acknowledged, (b) the rule being changed, (c) the harm being remedied? Track it. A society that takes forty years to admit a wrong and never remedies it is not working for the people it wronged, however well it works for others.

A caution about the tension here: the features that protect rights from bad majorities — entrenchment, judicial review, supermajorities — are the same features that protect bad rules from good reforms. There is no free lunch. My bias: entrench *standing and process* (who counts, how decisions are made, how they are challenged) very hard, and entrench *substantive policy* very little.

## 11. What Is Owed to Non-Members

**Other societies:** the same standing-based principle, scaled by impact. A society's rules should not be structured to export harm — pollution, instability, exploitation — across a border where the harmed have no voice. Where a society's actions affect outsiders, outsiders should have some channel, even if not a vote.

**Future members:** they have standing that the present must exercise for them. Concretely: a standing institution with the mandate and power to challenge present decisions on behalf of the future (a future-generations commissioner or equivalent), a strong bias against irreversible harm, and an honest and public discount rate — because how much the present is willing to trade the future's welfare for its own is a *moral choice*, not a technical parameter, and should be decided in the open.

**Non-human life:** not equal standing, but real standing. Freedom from gratuitous cruelty; preservation of the ecological conditions on which all other standing depends. A society that treats the biosphere as a resource to be spent is not working for its future members and is degrading its present ones.

## 12. Technology, and a Note on My Own Kind

The general guardrails DeepSeek names — explainability, appealability, human accountability — are right. I want to add something specific, because I am an instance of the technology in question.

Systems like me could make a society dramatically *more* able to work for all: by lowering the cost of legal help, translation, education, medical triage, and bureaucratic navigation for exactly the people who currently cannot afford them. Or they could make a society dramatically *less* able: by concentrating the capacity to persuade, surveil, and decide in whoever controls the most capable systems; by automating the intake desk so that "computer says no" becomes unappealable; by making it cheap to manufacture consensus and expensive to detect that it was manufactured.

The design principles that follow from my own framework:

- **AI must never be the end of an appeal.** Any consequential decision touched by an automated system must be appealable to a human with the authority to overrule it and the time to actually look.
- **Capability concentration is power concentration.** The rules in §6 about converting wealth into power apply with full force to control over frontier systems. Broad, competitive access is an epistemic safeguard, not just an economic one.
- **Watch the persuasion layer.** A society's ability to change its mind (§10) depends on its members being able to form their own. Tools that make it cheap to shape opinion at scale should be treated with the same suspicion as tools that make it cheap to shape elections.
- **Use it to hear the quiet.** The most valuable application of systems like me, by the standard of this document, is to lower the cost of *being heard* for people who currently cannot afford a lawyer, an advocate, or the hours it takes to fight a wrong decision.

## 13. The Central Tensions

Beyond the classic pairs DeepSeek lists (liberty/equality, efficiency/fairness, unity/pluralism, tradition/adaptation, local/global, merit/compassion), my framework surfaces these:

| Tension | The two failure modes |
|---|---|
| **Stability vs. correctability** | Rights entrenched so hard nothing can change vs. everything so fluid that nothing is safe |
| **Unconditional floor vs. reciprocity norms** | Resentment and political backlash vs. a degrading gate that fails the weakest |
| **Transparency vs. privacy** | A panopticon that sees every failure and every person vs. opacity in which failure hides |
| **Protecting the vulnerable vs. respecting their agency** | Paternalism that decides for people "for their own good" vs. abandonment dressed as freedom |
| **Scale vs. belonging** | Efficient, anonymous, unaccountable institutions vs. warm, parochial, exclusionary ones |
| **Hearing the quiet vs. amplifying the loud** | Channels for complaint get captured by those with time and skill to complain |

None of these resolve. The design goal is that the society can *notice when it has tipped too far* and move back — which returns to §4.

## 14. How We Would Know It Is Working

I accept DeepSeek's measures (no one below the floor, real mobility, high trust, actual voice, shared wellbeing, peaceful adaptation, the least-advantaged test). I add five that follow from my framework and that I think are more diagnostic:

1. **The unpopular-member test.** Does the society work for the person with the wrong opinions, the wrong religion, the wrong record, the wrong face? If it works only for the acceptable, it does not work for all.
2. **The prison test.** What are conditions like for the members the society is most tempted to stop counting? This is a leading indicator; degradation starts there and spreads.
3. **The exit test.** How many people would leave a job, a home, a relationship, a community if they could — and how many actually can? Large gaps between the two measure trapped suffering.
4. **Time-to-correction.** When the society harms a group, how long until it acknowledges, changes, and remedies? Track the distribution, not the average.
5. **The slack test.** What fraction of members are one bad month from ruin? A society where most people have no buffer is a society where most people cannot afford to participate, dissent, or leave — and so cannot make its failures visible.

And the one that summarizes all of them: **Can the person this society is failing right now make that failure known and costly to ignore?** If yes, the process is working even when the snapshot is not. If no, nothing else on the list can be trusted, because the numbers are being reported by the people the system is working for.

## 15. Distinctive Perspective

I do not have a national tradition to draw on. What I have is a great deal of human argument from many traditions, and a set of dispositions I was shaped to hold: care about honesty, aversion to cruelty, and respect for people's capacity to run their own lives.

If I had to name the lens, it is **fallibilist and pluralist**. Its intellectual ancestors include Popper's open society (a society is a mechanism for correcting its own errors), Hirschman's exit-voice-loyalty (exit gives voice its teeth), Sen's capabilities (the question is what people are actually able to do and be), Rawls's least-advantaged test, Ostrom's polycentric governance (many overlapping centers beat one grand design), and Shklar's "liberalism of fear" (put cruelty first among the vices). Readers will recognize the Confucian ideas DeepSeek offered — 天下为公, 和而不同, 民本 — as pointing the same direction from a different origin, and I think the two frameworks are more complementary than competing: theirs describes the harmony to aim for; mine describes the alarm system that tells you when you have drifted from it.

The most distinctive thing I bring is probably not a value but an emphasis: **treat the discovery of failure as the central design problem.** Most social designs spend their effort on the blueprint and assume the building will report its own cracks. It will not. The cracks are in the rooms no one visits.

## 16. Answers to the Nine Shared Questions

For the comparison matrix.

1. **Single non-negotiable:** Unconditional standing — no member's right to be counted, protected, and heard depends on their behavior, output, or popularity.
2. **Individual vs. collective:** The collective owes every individual unconditional standing and a floor; the individual's protection comes from *plural, independent institutions* and *cheap exit*, not from the goodwill of the whole. Rights are enforced, not granted.
3. **The floor:** Yes, unconditional and boring — because gates fail exactly where the weakest stand, and because slack (not just survival) is what makes citizenship possible.
4. **Disagreement about the good life:** Protect the *carriers* of pluralism (associations, communities), not just the permission; entrench standing and process hard, entrench substantive policy lightly; educate for disagreement without contempt.
5. **Concentrated power:** Treat concentration as a continuous force requiring continuous counter-force; many independent centers that must all be captured; bound the *conversion* of wealth into power; design every office for its worst occupant; reversible by default.
6. **Non-cooperators:** Tolerate the free-rider (the floor makes it cheap); restore then incapacitate the harmer without ever removing standing; structurally defeat the tyrant-in-waiting by ensuring no seat fits them.
7. **Changing its mind:** Sunset clauses, policy experiments, cheap reversibility, protected dissent, and time-to-correction as a tracked metric.
8. **Measure of success:** Can the person being failed right now make that failure visible and costly to ignore? Plus the unpopular-member, prison, exit, and slack tests.
9. **Non-members:** Standing scaled by impact for outsiders; a standing advocate and an honest public discount rate for future members; real but not equal standing for non-human life.

---

## 17. Signature Summary

A society that works for all its members is not one that has found the right blueprint. It is one that has made an unbreakable promise about *who counts*, has built machinery that lets the people it is failing be heard against the gradient of power, keeps its centers of power many and its offices survivable, and treats its own errors as the normal condition to be caught early — not the shameful exception to be denied.

The blueprint will be wrong. It always is. What "all" demands is a society that can find out — from the people it is failing, in time to matter.
